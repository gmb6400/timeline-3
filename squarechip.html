Origin Pre-1800s

A Limited Native Range

Before Osage-orange became familiar across Iowa and the Midwest, its native range was much smaller. Iowa State University Extension places it primarily in eastern Texas, southeastern Oklahoma, and southwestern Arkansas. The species was therefore not originally widespread across the central United States. Its much larger modern distribution came later, as people deliberately moved and planted the tree for agricultural purposes.

Sources: Iowa State University Extension and Outreach; U.S. Forest Service

Promotion 1841-1847

Promoted as a Living Fence

By the 1840s, agricultural writers and reformers were actively promoting Osage-orange as a solution to the prairie fencing problem. In January 1841, Prairie Farmer editor John S. Wright began advocating for the tree as a hedge plant, and Jonathan B. Turner later promoted it in Illinois. Because prairie farms often lacked inexpensive timber for conventional fencing, a dense living hedge offered an appealing alternative. Their efforts helped launch a period of widespread enthusiasm for Osage-orange fencing.

Source: U.S. Forest Service

Spread 1850s-1870s

Planted Across the Midwest

By the 1850s, enthusiasm for Osage-orange hedges had grown into a regional industry. Nurseries in Arkansas and Texas shipped seeds and young plants north to prairie states, while contractors planted and maintained hedges for farmers, railroads and landowners. In spring 1855 alone, about 9,000 miles of Osage-orange hedge were planted. By 1868, the seed trade in Illinois, Indiana and Ohio totaled 18,000 bushels, showing how quickly the tree had spread far beyond its original range.

Source: U.S. Forest Service

Iowa 1857

Hedging Takes Hold in Iowa

Osage-orange quickly became useful on Iowa’s largely treeless prairie, where settlers needed fencing but often lacked enough timber for traditional wooden fences. An 1857 Davis County agricultural report said hedging was attracting “a large share of attention” and recorded about 11 miles of hedge already growing in the county. Osage-orange became Iowa’s most successful substitute fencing material until barbed wire began replacing living hedges in the 1870s.

Sources: Iowa Conservationist; Iowa Official Register

Change 1870s-1895

Barbed Wire Replaces Living Hedges

The spread of barbed wire changed the role of Osage-orange on Midwestern farms. Beginning in the 1870s, farmers gained access to a cheaper and easier way to fence livestock without the years of planting, pruning and maintenance required by a living hedge. In Iowa, barbed wire was becoming widespread by 1885, and hedge removal was already underway by 1895. Although farmers no longer needed Osage-orange as their primary fence, its durable wood remained valuable for fence posts.

Sources: Iowa Conservationist; U.S. Forest Service

Legacy Today

A Lasting Agricultural Legacy

Barbed wire put an end to the boom in living hedges, but it didn't get rid of the Osage-orange from the landscape. There are still some of the original hedge trees to be found in fence rows in southern Iowa according to the Iowa State University Extension. The species has naturalized in pastures and ravines as well. Elsewhere you can still find old plantings in fence rows, on roadsides and on old farmsteads. The surviving trees and the hedgeballs they drop every fall are the tangible legacy of the way 19th-century farming helped to alter the range of the tree.

Sources: Iowa State University Extension and Outreach; U.S. Forest Service
